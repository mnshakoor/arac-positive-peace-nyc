On one side of a New York street, Sierra Leoneans came to celebrate their president. On the other, fellow Sierra Leoneans came to challenge him. Both groups had crossed an ocean, in the political sense if not literally that morning, to make themselves heard. Both claimed to speak for a country they care about. And both, in different ways, asked the world to pay attention.

This is the picture that emerges from the video report **“Sierra Leone: President Bio's UNGA Rally Met with Both Celebration and Protests.”** The footage, published on September 29, 2025, documents competing Sierra Leonean diaspora voices during the 80th United Nations General Assembly (UNGA80). The rally and counter-demonstration unfolded in the orbit of the United Nations General Assembly, where international diplomacy takes place behind guarded doors and national arguments find another stage outside them.

It is tempting to treat such scenes as a contest of slogans, flags and crowd sizes. Who turned out more people? Which message was louder? Which side had the better signs? But those questions tell us comparatively little about Sierra Leone's future.

The more important question is this: **What happens to a democracy when its citizens, including those living abroad, no longer agree on whether the institutions meant to settle political disagreements can be trusted?**

## A street in New York, a political argument in Sierra Leone

The 2025 footage presents a familiar yet revealing contrast. Supporters of President Julius Maada Bio express confidence in his leadership and point to national progress. Critics voice dissatisfaction over elections, accountability, civil liberties, economic hardship and the country's devastating drug problem.

Those positions deserve to be heard as *claims and perspectives*, not automatically converted into independently verified findings. A demonstration can establish that people hold a grievance, and that they are motivated to publicize it. It cannot, on its own, settle the truth of each allegation or represent the opinions of all Sierra Leoneans.

Still, dismissing the gathering as diaspora noise would be a serious analytical mistake. People who leave a country do not necessarily leave its political and emotional life. They send money, support relatives, follow elections, organize communities and attempt to influence decisions from abroad. Distance can change their vantage point without diminishing their stake.

New York's UNGA week makes that connection unusually visible. The same city that receives heads of state also gives citizens and expatriates a place to contest the stories their governments tell about themselves.

## Why the election dispute still matters

The controversy over Sierra Leone's June 2023 elections did not simply evaporate once official results were announced. The opposition All People's Congress challenged the outcome, and subsequent negotiations produced an Agreement for National Unity and a cross-party review of electoral systems and management bodies. That process was not merely symbolic. Its mandate explicitly addressed disputed election management practices, verification, transparency and confidence in future elections [1].

International IDEA's assessment of Sierra Leone identified continuing concerns about electoral administration, including voter-registration procedures and the perceived independence of the Electoral Commission [2]. By 2025, the reform agenda had generated proposed constitutional and legislative changes, but implementation delays remained a concern [3]. A 2026 joint assessment by international guarantors also emphasized timelines for restructuring the Electoral Commission ahead of the 2028 elections [4].

This history complicates the easy binary of government loyalist versus opposition protester. There is a documented institutional reform agenda, and it offers a practical way to judge progress: not by applause, and not by accusation alone, but by changes that citizens can inspect.

Will electoral information become easier to verify? Will contested processes be governed by rules that all major participants can accept? Will institutions demonstrate independence in practice, rather than merely claim it? Those are the kinds of questions that build or erode public confidence long after an election day.

## The young people missing from political talking points

Among the issues raised in the original account of the demonstrations was **kush**, a drug crisis that has affected young Sierra Leoneans and their families. Here the argument should not collapse into another exercise in partisan blame.

Afrobarometer reported in 2025 that 96 percent of respondents had heard of kush and that, among those aware of it, 99 percent considered its abuse a significant national problem [5]. In August 2026, the United Nations in Sierra Leone reported that authorities said 1,071 young people had completed rehabilitation since the public-health emergency declaration [6].

Both facts matter. One shows how widely the problem is recognized. The other points to a response that merits examination and sustained support. Neither proves that enforcement alone can solve the crisis. And neither justifies treating those living with addiction as disposable casualties of political conflict.

The deeper issue is human capital: what chances do young people have to learn, work, stay healthy and imagine a future worth investing in? A country can win a political argument and still lose a generation. That is a far more consequential failure than an embarrassing demonstration abroad.

## Positive Peace is not the absence of protest

At ARAC International, we use the Institute for Economics & Peace's **Positive Peace** framework to examine the attitudes, institutions and structures that allow societies to flourish. Its eight interconnected pillars help explain why peace is more demanding than the absence of open violence [7].

Several pillars are especially relevant to the Sierra Leonean divide.

**Free Flow of Information.** Citizens need credible reporting, accessible public records and the means to question official narratives. Equally, political actors and activists have a responsibility not to amplify unverified allegations as established facts. Information integrity is not censorship; it is the discipline of separating evidence from assertion.

**Acceptance of the Rights of Others.** A supporter of the president does not lose the right to speak because an opponent distrusts the government. An opponent does not lose the right to protest because a supporter considers the criticism unfair. Rights become meaningful precisely when they protect people with whom we disagree.

**Well-Functioning Government and Low Levels of Corruption.** Trust cannot be commanded into existence. It develops when elections are credible, public funds are accounted for, institutions respond to criticism and the rules apply consistently. Allegations should be investigated fairly, without presuming guilt or using due process as a pretext for impunity.

**High Levels of Human Capital.** Public health, education, youth opportunity and pathways out of addiction are not distractions from political reform. They are among the conditions that make democratic life more resilient.

These pillars interact. An information environment flooded with suspicion can weaken trust in institutions. Weak institutions can deepen grievances. Economic exclusion can make political anger more combustible. And political polarization can make even sensible reforms harder to implement.

This is why Positive Peace is a *systems* approach. We should not ask only how to quiet a protest. We should ask what conditions would make citizens more confident that they can obtain answers, accountability and fair treatment without having to shout across barriers.

## Diaspora activism: bridge or amplifier?

Diaspora participation offers a genuine opportunity for peacebuilding. Sierra Leoneans abroad can connect local concerns to international networks, professional expertise and civic resources. They can raise money for services, advocate for transparent reforms and help communities understand one another across party lines.

But diaspora politics also carries a risk. When politics is mediated through social media clips, activist channels and tightly bonded partisan communities, the loudest voices can appear more representative than they are. Outrage travels faster than verification. A complicated dispute becomes an accusation; a legitimate criticism becomes an attack on identity; an ordinary political opponent becomes an enemy.

The answer is not to demand that diaspora communities be neutral. They have the same right to hold political convictions as anyone else. The challenge is to practice **principled disagreement without civic exclusion**.

That means asking supporters to acknowledge unresolved grievances, asking critics to distinguish substantiated abuses from allegations, and asking everyone to judge progress by outcomes affecting ordinary people. It also means making space for Sierra Leoneans whose concerns do not fit neatly into the ruling-party-versus-opposition frame.

A grandmother worrying about the price of essentials, a student seeking a job and a family caring for someone with substance-use disorder may have little patience for diaspora factional theater. Their needs should help set the terms of debate.

## From international spectacle to measurable reform

What, then, would constructive engagement look like?

First, follow the electoral reform commitments and publish understandable progress updates. The public should be able to see what was recommended, what has changed, what remains disputed and who is responsible. Second, insist on independent and fair mechanisms for handling rights complaints and corruption allegations, rather than treating them as weapons of partisan competition. Third, give the kush crisis sustained public-health attention alongside effective action against criminal supply networks. Finally, encourage diaspora forums where people with opposing views can question one another without intimidation or humiliation.

These are not substitutes for domestic political leadership. External advocates cannot manufacture legitimacy for Sierra Leone's institutions. But they can support a culture in which claims are tested, reforms are tracked and civic participation is not reserved for the politically agreeable.

There is also a lesson here for those of us observing from New York. We should resist the urge to turn a complex country's democratic struggles into an easily shareable confrontation. A camera shows an important moment. It does not show the whole society.

## Two flags, one responsibility

The people gathered near the United Nations may have disagreed about President Bio, the meaning of the 2023 election and the direction of the country. Yet their very presence pointed to something they shared: Sierra Leone mattered enough for them to speak in public.

That shared concern is not reconciliation, and it should not be romanticized. Genuine divisions can involve serious questions of rights, evidence and power. The goal is not to erase those divisions or declare all claims equally valid. It is to build institutions capable of handling disagreement without turning every dispute into a crisis of belonging.

The final measure of a nation's stability is not whether its citizens praise the government. Nor is it whether dissent disappears from the streets. It is whether those citizens, supporters and opponents alike, can trust that their voices count, their rights are protected and the country's future is not the private property of any political faction.

**A democracy does not become stronger when everyone agrees. It becomes stronger when disagreement no longer threatens the social fabric that holds its people together.**
